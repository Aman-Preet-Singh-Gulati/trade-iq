The yield curve — specifically the spread between the 10-year and 2-year Treasury yields — has preceded every U.S. recession of the last fifty years. That track record is exactly why it deserves scrutiny rather than blind faith: the signal is real, but the timing is far looser than most headlines suggest.

## What Inversion Actually Means

Under normal conditions, longer-dated bonds yield more than shorter-dated ones, compensating investors for locking up capital for longer. An inversion means the market expects short-term rates to fall — usually because it expects the central bank to be cutting in response to a weakening economy. It is a forward-looking signal about *expectations*, not a real-time economic indicator.

## The Lag Is the Hard Part

Historically, the lag between inversion and the onset of recession has ranged from roughly 6 to 24 months. That range is wide enough that positioning a portfolio purely on "the curve inverted, sell everything" has burned traders who front-ran a recession that was still a year and a half away, missing a substantial equity rally in the meantime.

## What to Actually Watch Alongside the Curve

- **Un-inversion, not inversion**: recessions have historically tended to begin *after* the curve re-steepens, not while it is still inverted.
- **Credit spreads**: widening high-yield spreads alongside an inverted curve is a stronger combined signal than either indicator alone.
- **Labor market breadth**: a broadening rise in unemployment claims tends to confirm what the curve is only suggesting.

## The Takeaway

Treat the yield curve as one input into a broader mosaic, not a standalone trading signal. Its historical reliability is genuine, but its timing precision is not — and trading a directionally-correct thesis with the wrong timing is still a losing trade.
