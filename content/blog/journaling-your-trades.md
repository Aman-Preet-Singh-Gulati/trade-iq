Ask any trader who has genuinely improved over multiple years how they did it, and a trade journal shows up in almost every answer. Not a P&L spreadsheet — a real journal that captures the reasoning behind each decision.

## Why Memory Isn't Enough

Human memory reconstructs the past to fit a comfortable narrative. Without a written record, it's remarkably easy to remember your good trades as "the real you" and your bad trades as unlucky exceptions — which means you never actually confront the patterns that are costing you money.

## What to Actually Record

- **The setup and why you took it**, in your own words, before you know the outcome.
- **Your emotional state** at entry — rushed, confident, revenge-trading, calm. This single field often explains more variance in results than any technical factor.
- **Whether you followed your own plan**, independent of whether the trade won or lost. This is the field that actually drives improvement.

## Reviewing Without Self-Deception

A weekly review should group trades by *process quality*, not by outcome. The goal is to find the handful of decision patterns that are costing you money regardless of any individual result — oversized entries after a loss, chasing a move you already missed, exiting winners early out of fear. These patterns are invisible without a written record and glaringly obvious once you have thirty entries to look back on.

Journaling is unglamorous and easy to skip on a busy day. It is also, empirically, one of the highest-leverage habits available to any trader serious about improving.
